Profile

A little about how I got here. The full story is on its own page.

Understanding people. Building trust. Creating value.

For as long as I can remember, I have wanted to know what happens underneath behavior: why we react before we reflect, how we form opinions, what makes us change our minds. Psychology turned that curiosity into a discipline, and a turning point as a teenager turned it into a purpose: putting that understanding in service of others.

I was raised in Madrid, continued my secondary education in England, and completed it in the United States before coming to Tulane in New Orleans, where I study psychology with minors in Strategy, Leadership & Analytics, AI Literacy, and French. Moving between cultures taught me to notice how differently people see the same situation.

My experience spans a developmental research lab at Tulane, applied behavior work, clinical observation, crisis support, bilingual community mental health, and earlier roles in business and customer service in Madrid.

In this portfolio I focus on the people a brand serves: how perception, identity, and culture shape what they choose, wear, and return to, in a shop, in a conversation, or on a screen. I have not held a sales or retail strategy job yet, and I will not pretend otherwise. What I bring is training in how people make decisions, real practice listening to people one to one, and years of following fashion closely.

Two linen blazers, two theories of the client

A teardown of two real product pages, read on 12 November 2026: a 100% linen blazer at Zara for $119 and one at Reformation for $278. Same garment, opposite beliefs about the person buying it.

What a product page can doZaraReformation

  • Zara

    One price, $119. Nothing struck through.

    Reformation

    One price, $278. Nothing struck through.

    How I read it

    The one thing they agree on. On a full-price page, a crossed-out number tells the client the real price is negotiable. Neither puts a markdown on this page, so this page never teaches anyone to wait.

  • Zara

    No reviews, no ratings, no counts. The only voice on the page is the brand’s.

    Reformation

    No star ratings in what I saw. Other buyers appear once, in a single line reporting that the style runs true to size.

    How I read it

    Zara asks me to trust its eye. Reformation borrows other clients for exactly one job: the doubt most likely to cause a return. That is social proof used as information, not as applause, and it is the most disciplined use of it I have seen on a product page.

  • Zara

    No stock message anywhere on the page.

    Reformation

    Stock is stated per size at the moment of choosing. On a suggested pair of shoes on the same page, sizes are marked low stock or waitlist.

    How I read it

    Zara is widely described as making short runs and replacing styles quickly, so scarcity may live in the assortment and never need a label. Reformation says it out loud, size by size, and turns a sold-out size into a waitlist, which makes a lost sale into a lead.

  • Zara

    One color, five sizes, and little else competing for attention in what I captured.

    Reformation

    One color for this piece, then three shelves of other pieces: complete the look, looks good with, and more blazers.

    How I read it

    Neither offers a wall of colors. But Reformation hands the choice back as an outfit, more than a dozen linked pieces. Its page is built to raise the size of the order. Zara’s is built to finish this one.

  • Zara

    Return terms sit behind a link. What the page offers is a table of the garment measured flat, and a button to check stock in a store.

    Reformation

    Free shipping is stated on the page. Then fit, three ways: how it is cut, the model’s height, measurements and size, and what buyers report.

    How I read it

    Both lower the risk, for different clients. Zara’s table serves someone who owns a tape measure and knows their numbers, or who will walk into a store. Reformation writes for someone deciding on a phone, alone, who needs to picture the piece on a body.

  • Zara

    The name is a description of the object, followed by a reference number. The copy lists the neck, the pockets, the hem, the buttons. The person is in the eight photographs, not in the words.

    Reformation

    The blazer has a first name, James. The copy jokes, and promises a fabric that “gets softer every time you wear it.” Then come flax, water, the factory, and what to do when you are done with it.

    How I read it

    This is where they split completely. Zara lets the image carry all of the identity and keeps the words out of the way. Reformation tells me who I am if I buy it: funny, and careful about what I own.

Zara: the client already wants it

The page behaves like a catalog entry wrapped around an editorial shoot. It assumes desire was created earlier, in a store window, a feed, or the photograph itself, and that the page’s only job is not to slow anyone down. Few words and one short button make it easy to process, and what is easy to process tends to feel right. In the language of persuasion research this is the peripheral route: the brand and the image decide, not the argument.

Petty & Cacioppo, Communication and Persuasion, 1986. Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman, “Processing Fluency and Aesthetic Pleasure,” Personality and Social Psychology Review, 2004

Reformation: the client has three doubts

Will it fit, is it worth $278, and is it all right to want it. The page answers in that order: fit information three ways, a fabric that improves with wear, then sustainability. That last block is doing more than ethics. People buy something they want more readily when they have a reason that makes it feel justified, and a lower footprint is a reason. The extra copy is carrying the $159 between the two prices.

Okada, “Justification Effects on Consumer Choice of Hedonic and Utilitarian Goods,” Journal of Marketing Research, 2005

What I would test at each

  • At Zara: one line of fit feedback from buyers, shown to first-time visitors only. Does it cut size-related returns without slowing the add to bag? My guess is that the measurement table serves the regular and loses the newcomer.
  • At Reformation: the sustainability block shown before the price for half of visitors and after it for the rest. Does it change who buys, or mainly how buyers feel a week later, and so whether they send it back?
  • At both: whether the client who arrives from a search needs a different page from the one who arrives from the brand’s own feed.

What I cannot see from outside

Conversion, returns, where visitors come from, and how the app differs from the site. This is one page from each brand, on the US sites, at phone width, on one day. A page is a brand’s hypothesis about its client. Only its own data can say whether the hypothesis holds, which is the part of this work I want to do next.

An independent reading of public pages. I am not affiliated with either brand and have no access to their data.

How I listen to a client

Selling well starts with hearing what was said. This is one short exchange on a shop floor, analyzed the way I would analyze an interview: from first read to something a retail team could act on.

Illustrative exchange, written for this page. It is not a real client.

A client comes into a store on a Thursday afternoon looking for a blazer.

  1. Client

    I’m looking for a blazer. I start a new job on Monday, and nothing I own feels right.

  2. Associate

    Congratulations. This linen one is the piece most people choose. It is $248.

  3. Client

    I saw one a lot like it somewhere else for about $150.

  4. Associate

    That one is probably unlined, which is where the difference goes. Try both on and you will feel it in the shoulders.

  5. Client

    Okay. My sister says I buy things and never wear them. Which one would you pick?

  6. Associate

    It depends on Monday. Which one would you reach for at seven in the morning?

  7. Client

    That’s the thing. I don’t want to look like I’m trying too hard.

Read it once, before any labels

I read the whole exchange first and write down what I notice in plain words. Labels come later, so that they do not decide what I hear.

  • There is a date attached to this purchase.
  • They question the price, then ask the associate to choose.
  • The last line is not about the blazer.

Three impressions. The next steps test whether they hold up.

Rules I code by

  • One code per sentence. Code what the sentence does in the conversation, not its topic.
  • Leave a sentence uncoded when no definition fits. Three of the eight here have no code.
  • Only the client’s sentences are coded. The associate’s turns are context.

The occasion

Use when
There is a specific event, date, or person.
Not when

What I would ask nextWhat do you want to be true about Monday at nine?

Identity at stake

Use when
“Feels like me,” “not me,” how others will read them.
Not when
Comments on fit, fabric, or color alone.

What I would ask nextWhen did something you own last feel right?

Price check

The client tests the price against evidence of their own.

Use when
They cite another price, store, or past purchase.
Not when
They only ask what it costs.

What I would ask nextWhat would the other one need to have for you to choose it?

Handing over the decision

The client asks the associate to choose for them.

Use when
They ask for a verdict on their own choice.
Not when
They ask for information or a comparison.

What I would ask nextIf I chose, would you trust it, or would you still wonder?

Restating the need

The client sets aside how they were understood and says what they actually need.

Use when
A correction followed by a restatement.
Not when
They simply change the subject.

What I would ask nextWhat would trying too hard look like? What would just right look like?
