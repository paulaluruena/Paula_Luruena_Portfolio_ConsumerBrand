Deeper work

For readers with more than two minutes: five moments in a client’s decision, two studies I would run for a brand, seven ideas from psychology about selling, and how I think a client conversation should go.

Five moments in a client’s decision

A purchase is five smaller decisions. For each one: what psychology says is happening, the numbers a retail team watches, how I would study it, and the question I would bring.

Attention is a choice the mind makes for us

The psychology

Before anyone weighs a brand, they have to register it. Attention is selective, and familiarity alone makes people like something more.

Zajonc, “Attitudinal Effects of Mere Exposure,” Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1968

What a retail team watches

  • Reach
  • Branded search
  • Share of new visitors
  • Unaided recall

How I would study it

Five-second tests of a window or a feed post, then unaided recall interviews ten minutes later.

The question I would bring

What do people remember ten minutes after seeing the window, and is it what the brand meant them to see?

In the lab I code what children choose to attend to. The discipline is the same: record what was noticed, not what was shown. Learning & Brain Development Lab

People shop for a version of themselves

The psychology

People lean toward brands whose image matches how they see themselves, or how they would like to be seen. A client deciding whether a brand is “for someone like me” is making an identity judgment before a product one.

Sirgy, “Self-Concept in Consumer Behavior,” Journal of Consumer Research, 1982

What a retail team watches

  • Product views per visit
  • Add-to-bag rate
  • Wishlist saves
  • Time to first purchase

How I would study it

Interviews built around one real moment: “show me the last thing you almost bought.” Then a card sort of who each brand is for.

The question I would bring

Who does the client think this brand is for, and do they count themselves in?

At Puentes I learned to ask about one concrete situation before any general question. People describe what they did far more accurately than what they usually do. Puentes New Orleans

Holding something makes it partly yours

The psychology

Touching an object, or vividly imagining owning it, raises how much people feel it is already theirs, and people ask more to give up something they own than they would pay to get it.

Peck & Shu, “The Effect of Mere Touch on Perceived Ownership,” Journal of Consumer Research, 2009. Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, Journal of Political Economy, 1990

What a retail team watches

  • Fitting-room conversion
  • Size-guide use
  • Bracketing rate
  • Return rate by reason

How I would study it

Fitting-room intercepts in store. Online, coding free-text return reasons into categories two people can agree on.

The question I would bring

What does the fitting room answer that the product page leaves open?

As a behavior technician I recorded what a child did before asking why. A return is a behavior with a reason code attached, and the code is usually the least informative part. Deep Roots Behavioral Support

Every extra step is a reason to stop

The psychology

A possible loss weighs more than an equal gain, so doubt grows as the moment of paying gets closer. Effort matters as much as price: making something easy does more for loyalty than trying to delight.

Kahneman & Tversky, Econometrica, 1979. Dixon, Freeman & Toman, “Stop Trying to Delight Your Customers,” Harvard Business Review, 2010

What a retail team watches

  • Checkout conversion
  • Cart abandonment
  • Average order value
  • Payment-step drop-off

How I would study it

An A/B test with a holdout group, paired with a one-question exit survey for people who leave the bag.

The question I would bring

At which step do people stop feeling sure, and is it the price or the effort?

In a restaurant in Madrid I watched a line form or dissolve depending on one slow step at the till. People forgive a price sooner than a wait. 100 Montaditos

People remember the peak and the end

The psychology

An experience is remembered by its most intense moment and by how it finished, not by its average. What happens after the purchase is where the memory is made.

Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier, Psychological Science, 1993. Reichheld, “The One Number You Need to Grow,” Harvard Business Review, 2003

What a retail team watches

  • Repeat purchase rate
  • Time to second order
  • Net Promoter Score
  • Customer lifetime value

How I would study it

Cohort analysis of first-time buyers, with follow-up interviews at thirty days with those who came back and those who did not.

The question I would bring

What happened between the first and the second purchase for the clients who returned?

On the Cruz Roja line, how a call ended decided whether the person would call again. I think about endings before I think about openings. Cruz Roja

Two studies I would like to run for a brand

Proposals, not completed studies. Each starts from a problem retail teams argue about and from something I have done with my own hands.

Three sizes, two returns

When a client orders three sizes to keep one, is that a sizing problem or a trust problem?

Where I am coming from

As a behavior technician I was trained to record what a person does before asking why. An order with three sizes of the same piece is a behavior. The return reason typed into a form afterward is a guess about it, usually a polite one.

The tension

Free returns take the risk out of buying, which is why they work: a possible loss weighs more than an equal gain. But a client who orders the same piece in three sizes is saying the product page did not let them predict what would arrive. The brand pays for that uncertainty twice, in shipping and in stock that is out of reach while it travels. Tightening the returns policy treats the symptom and puts the risk back on the client.

What psychology predicts

  1. It is uncertainty, not habit. Ordering several sizes should cluster in first orders and in categories the client has not bought from before, and fade once they know how the brand fits.
  2. People like me beat a size chart. When people are unsure they look to others like them. Fit notes from buyers with a similar height and usual size should cut multi-size orders more than a chart of measurements.
  3. Trying at home builds attachment. People value what already feels like theirs. Clients who order several sizes and keep one should reorder more often than clients who order one and send it back.

Design

Three steps. First, describe: classify a season of orders as single-size or multi-size, and compare first orders with repeat orders across categories. Second, listen: fifteen interviews with clients who recently ordered several sizes, built around that one order. Third, test: in one category, show half of visitors fit notes from similar buyers and half the standard size chart.

What I would measure

The share of orders with more than one size, the return rate, the share of clients who keep at least one piece, and whether they order again within ninety days. The primary outcome is the multi-size rate among first-time buyers.

What would change my mind

If long-standing clients order several sizes as often as new ones, in categories they know well, then it is not uncertainty. They are using home as the fitting room, and the answer is logistics, not information.

What a team could do with it

A view of which categories create the most doubt. A reason to invest in fit information before tightening returns. A way to tell the client who needs help choosing from the one who was always going to keep one.

Care and limits

Order data shows what people did, not why, which is what the interviews are for. One category in one season cannot speak for the whole range. I have not worked with retail order data yet; this is how I would approach it, and I would expect the first week to teach me what I have wrong.

Grounding: Kahneman & Tversky, “Prospect Theory,” Econometrica, 1979. Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, “Experimental Tests of the Endowment Effect and the Coase Theorem,” Journal of Political Economy, 1990. Cialdini, Influence, 1984. Draws on behavioral data collection at Deep Roots Behavioral Support and one-to-one interviews at Puentes New Orleans.

What a sale teaches

Does a discount win this purchase and teach the client to wait for the next one?

Where I am coming from

In applied behavior analysis, what you reward, and when, is what you get more of. A markdown is a reward delivered on a schedule, and people learn schedules quickly. I spent a summer watching that happen one session at a time.

The tension

People do not judge a price on its own. They judge it against the price they expect, and that expectation is built from the prices they have seen before. Research on reference prices has found this again and again. A promotion lifts sales this week and lowers the expected price a little, and a brand that promotes often may be teaching its best clients that the full price is for people who did not wait.

What psychology predicts

  1. The expected price drifts. After two visits that include a promotion, the price people say they expect to pay should sit closer to the promotional price than to the full one.
  2. Waiting is learned. People who first met the brand through a promotion should be less willing to pay full price later than people who first met it at full price, for the same piece.
  3. Not every reward is a discount. A reward that is not a lower price, such as early access to a collection, should bring people back without moving the price they expect.

Design

A survey experiment with about 300 people who shop for clothing online. Each sees the same invented brand across three simulated visits a week apart. They are randomly assigned one of three histories: always full price, two promotions, or two early-access invitations. On the fourth visit everyone sees the same piece at full price.

What I would measure

The price they expect to pay, asked before the price is shown. Whether they would buy now or wait. How they describe the brand in three words. The primary outcome is the gap between the expected price and the full price, by history.

What would change my mind

If the expected price does not move after two promotions, clients are reading a sale as an event, not as the real price, and the concern is overstated for brands that promote rarely.

What a team could do with it

Expected price as a number worth tracking beside sales. Evidence for or against rewarding loyal clients with access in place of discounts. A reason to look at promotion frequency per client, not only per season.

Care and limits

Simulated visits are not real spending, and three weeks is short. Luxury and mass brands will differ, since for some houses never discounting is part of what is being bought. A brand with real purchase histories could check the second prediction directly, and that would be the better study.

Grounding: Kalyanaram & Winer, “Empirical Generalizations from Reference Price Research,” Marketing Science, 1995. Tversky & Kahneman, “Judgment under Uncertainty,” Science, 1974. Draws on reinforcement procedures and session-by-session data collection at Deep Roots Behavioral Support.

Psychology & the client

Seven ideas from psychology that explain, to me, what good selling is. The research is established. How I apply it to sales is my own reading.

  • People decide with more than logic

    The psychology

    Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that the way a choice is framed changes what people choose, and that a loss weighs more heavily than an equal gain.

    Kahneman & Tversky, “Prospect Theory,” Econometrica, 1979

    In a sales conversation

    A client is weighing risk as much as price. Understanding what they are afraid of getting wrong is often more useful than adding one more feature to the list.

  • Trust has three parts

    The psychology

    Research on trust separates it into ability, benevolence, and integrity: whether you know what you are doing, whether you care about my interests, and whether you keep your word.

    Mayer, Davis & Schoorman, Academy of Management Review, 1995

    In a sales conversation

    Knowing the product is only the first part. A client also has to believe I am looking out for them, and to see me do what I said I would.

    Built onPuentes New OrleansCruz Roja

  • Listening is a skill, not a pause

    The psychology

    Carl Rogers argued that people open up and change when they feel accurately understood, and that a listener shows it by reflecting back what was said before responding.

    Rogers, Journal of Consulting Psychology, 1957

    In a sales conversation

    The first answer a client gives is rarely the whole need. I ask one more question, then repeat back what I heard so they can correct me.

    Built onRiverway Psychological HealthMontalur

  • Pressure backfires

    The psychology

    People commit to choices they feel are their own. When they feel pushed, they tend to push back, an effect psychologists call reactance.

    Brehm, A Theory of Psychological Reactance, 1966. Ryan & Deci, American Psychologist, 2000

    In a sales conversation

    Persuasion works through understanding. I would rather give a client clear information and room to decide: a yes they chose lasts longer than a yes they were pressed into.

  • Emotion is information

    The psychology

    Emotional intelligence is the ability to perceive emotions accurately, in yourself and in others, and to use them to guide thinking and action.

    Salovey & Mayer, Imagination, Cognition and Personality, 1990

    In a sales conversation

    Hesitation, relief, or irritation often says what a client has not put into words. Noticing it lets me slow down, clarify, or change direction.

    Built onCruz RojaDeep Roots Behavioral Support

  • People remember the peak and the end

    The psychology

    We do not remember an experience as an average. Memory is shaped mostly by its most intense moment and by how it finished.

    Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier, Psychological Science, 1993

    In a sales conversation

    How a conversation closes, and what happens after the purchase, decide whether a customer comes back. Follow-up is part of the sale.

    Built on100 Montaditos

  • What people do says more than what they say

    The psychology

    Behavioral research starts from observation: define the behavior, record it consistently, and let the data correct your assumptions.

    Research training, Tulane University

    In a sales conversation

    A sales team runs on the same habit. Which conversations lead somewhere, where clients drop off, and what changed after a new approach are questions I am trained to ask with data.

My approach

How I think selling should work, in five stages. It is a developing philosophy built on psychology and on serving people one to one. I have not yet practiced it in a sales role.

ListenUnderstandIdentify valueCommunicateBuild trust

Start with the person

Before I say anything about what I can offer, I want to know who I am talking to.

Open questions, one at a time, and enough silence for a real answer. Then I repeat back what I heard, so the client can correct me before I build on a misunderstanding.

The psychology behind it

  • Feeling accurately understood is what makes people open up.
  • Open questions bring out needs that yes-or-no questions hide.

Find the need under the request

What someone asks for and what they need are often not the same thing.

A budget, a deadline, a manager to convince, a past purchase that went badly: the reasons behind a request decide what will actually help. I look for what the client is trying to get done.

The psychology behind it

  • Motivation explains behavior better than stated preference does.
  • People weigh a possible loss more heavily than an equal gain.

Match, don’t push

Value is where what the client needs meets what I can honestly offer.

If the fit is real, I should be able to say exactly why. If it is not, saying so is worth more than a sale the client will regret, and it is the reason they will take my call next time.

The psychology behind it

  • Integrity is one of the three foundations of trust.
  • People stay committed to decisions they feel they made freely.

Say it so it lands

The same offer has to be explained differently to different people.

Plain language, the client’s own words, and the two or three points that matter to them. Some people want the numbers first and others want the story, and it is my job to notice which.

The psychology behind it

  • Framing changes how the same information is received.
  • Culture and language shape what sounds clear, polite, or convincing.

Keep the relationship after the yes

Trust is built by what happens after someone agrees.

Following up, doing what I said I would, and being easy to reach when something goes wrong. Repeat business and referrals come from how a relationship is kept, which is why I count this stage as part of selling.

The psychology behind it

  • People remember how an experience ended.
  • Consistency over time is how reliability is judged.
